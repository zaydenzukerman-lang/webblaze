Turning 26: What Happens to Your Health Insurance When You Age Off a Parent's Plan

6 min read · Updated October 6, 2026

Federal law lets you stay on a parent's health plan until you turn 26 — whether or not you're married, a student, financially dependent, or living at home. After that, you need coverage of your own. The rule is simple; the timing is what catches people.

The short version

  • Find your exact end date — it isn't always your birthday.
  • Losing a parent's coverage opens a Special Enrollment Period, so you don't have to wait for Open Enrollment.
  • You can usually apply up to 60 days before coverage ends, which lets you avoid any gap.
  • If the parent's plan is through a larger employer, you can usually keep the same plan on COBRA for up to 36 months — at full price.

When does coverage actually end?

Plans must make dependent coverage available until you turn 26, but each plan sets the exact cutoff. Some end coverage on your 26th birthday, some at the end of that month, and some at the end of the plan or calendar year. Ask the parent whose plan you're on to check with their HR department or insurer for the specific date — and get it in writing.

Florida has its own rule worth knowing: some insured plans must allow certain dependents to stay on until age 30 if they're unmarried, have no dependents of their own, live in Florida or are full-time students, and have no other coverage. It doesn't apply to every plan — many large employers' self-funded plans aren't subject to it — so ask the plan directly.

Your options after 26

  • Your own employer's plan. If your job offers group coverage, this is usually the simplest route — check how long your new-hire waiting period is compared with your cutoff date.
  • COBRA. Aging off a parent's employer plan is a COBRA qualifying event, so you can usually keep the exact same coverage for up to 36 months. You'll pay the full premium (up to 102% of the plan's cost), with no employer share. You or your parent must notify the plan within 60 days. COBRA applies to employers with 20 or more employees; many states have similar rules for smaller employers.
  • A Marketplace plan. Losing coverage gives you a 60-day Special Enrollment Period before and after the loss. You're now your own household for subsidies, so your income — not your parents' — decides what you might qualify for.
  • A private PPO plan. Some people in good health compare a medically underwritten private plan alongside the Marketplace. Approval and pricing depend on your health history, and benefits, exclusions, and pre-existing-condition rules vary by plan, so compare the plan documents side by side.
  • Medicaid, depending on your state and income.

A step-by-step plan

  1. About 3 months before your birthday: confirm the exact date your coverage ends.
  2. Check whether you have your own employer coverage, and when it would start.
  3. If COBRA is available, ask for the monthly cost so you have a real number to compare.
  4. Within 60 days before your coverage ends: apply for a Marketplace plan or compare other options, so your new plan starts the day after the old one ends.
  5. Confirm your new effective date in writing before your old coverage stops.

What if I miss the window?

If more than 60 days pass after you lose coverage, you may have to wait for the next Marketplace Open Enrollment (November 1 to January 15 on HealthCare.gov). Medically underwritten private plans can be applied for year-round, but approval isn't guaranteed. The easiest fix is to start early.

Common questions

Can I stay on my parent's plan if I'm married or not a student? Yes. Until 26, marriage, student status, where you live, and whether you're financially dependent don't matter.

Can my parent's plan drop me the day I turn 26? It can, if that's how the plan is written. Many plans keep you through the end of the month or year, but you have to check.

Is COBRA worth it at 26? It keeps your doctors and deductible progress the same, which can matter mid-treatment. But you pay the full premium, so it's often more expensive than a Marketplace plan, especially if you qualify for a subsidy.

Do I need to do anything if I'm starting a job with benefits? Enroll as soon as you're eligible, and check whether there's a waiting period that leaves a gap after your parent's coverage ends.
