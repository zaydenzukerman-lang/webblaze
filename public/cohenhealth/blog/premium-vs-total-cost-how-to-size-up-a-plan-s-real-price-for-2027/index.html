Plan Comparisons

Premium vs. Total Cost: How to Size Up a Plan's Real Price for 2027

6 min read · Updated October 19, 2026

When you start shopping for next year's health coverage, one number jumps out first: the monthly premium. It is easy to sort plans from lowest to highest and pick the one at the top. But the premium is only part of what you pay over a year. A plan that looks cheap each month can end up costing more if you need care, and a plan with a higher premium can sometimes save you money if you see doctors often.

With fall shopping season here, this is a good time to learn a simple way to compare plans by their total yearly cost, not just their monthly bill. You do not need to be good at math. You just need a rough picture of your health needs and a few key numbers from each plan.

The Pieces That Make Up Your Real Cost

Every health plan has several cost parts that work together. Once you know what each one means, plan comparisons get much easier.

  • Premium: what you pay every month to keep your coverage, whether you use it or not.
  • Deductible: what you pay for many covered services before the plan starts sharing the cost.
  • Copay: a set amount you pay for certain visits or prescriptions, like a doctor visit or a generic drug.
  • Coinsurance: your share of the cost after you meet the deductible, shown as a portion of the bill.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum: the most you would pay for covered, in-network care in a plan year. After you reach it, the plan generally pays for covered services for the rest of the year.

Think of it this way. The premium is your fixed cost. Everything else is your variable cost, which depends on how much care you use. Your true yearly cost is the premiums you pay plus whatever you spend on care.

One important note: some services, such as many preventive care visits, may be covered before you meet your deductible on many plans. Each plan is different, so always check the plan details.

Picture Three Possible Years

Nobody knows exactly how much care they will need next year. That is why it helps to imagine three different years and see how each plan holds up.

  1. A quiet year: You stay healthy and only go in for a checkup. Here, your cost is mostly your premiums. Lower-premium plans usually look best in this case.
  2. An expected year: Think about what you normally use. Maybe a few doctor visits, a monthly prescription, or regular visits with a specialist. Add up your premiums plus your likely copays and costs.
  3. A tough year: Imagine an unexpected surgery, a hospital stay, or a new diagnosis. In this case, you may hit your out-of-pocket maximum. Your cost would be your yearly premiums plus that maximum.

The tough-year number is worth looking at closely. It tells you the most you could reasonably expect to pay for covered, in-network care in a bad year. Some people are comfortable with a higher worst case in exchange for a lower monthly bill. Others sleep better knowing their worst case is lower, even if it means a higher premium.

Here is a made-up example to show how this works. Imagine two plans. Plan A has a low premium but a high deductible. Plan B has a higher premium but a low deductible and lower copays. If you rarely see a doctor, Plan A may cost less over the year. If you have a chronic condition with frequent visits and prescriptions, Plan B may end up costing less overall, even with the bigger monthly payment. The right answer depends on you.

Make a Simple List of Your Health Needs

Before you compare plans, spend a few minutes writing down what care you and your family members might use next year. This makes your estimates much more realistic.

  • How many times did you see a primary care doctor this year?
  • Do you see any specialists regularly?
  • What prescriptions do you take, and how often do you refill them?
  • Are you planning anything big, like a surgery, having a baby, or starting therapy?
  • Do your kids tend to need urgent care visits during the school year?
  • Do you have any ongoing conditions that need lab work or imaging?

You do not need exact numbers. A rough list is enough to see whether you lean toward a quiet year or a busier one. If you are covering a family, think about each person separately, since many family plans have both individual and family deductibles and out-of-pocket limits.

Costs That Are Easy to Miss

Some of the biggest surprises in health care come from things that do not show up in the main plan summary. Watch for these as you compare.

First, networks matter. The cost-sharing numbers you see usually apply to in-network care. If your doctor or hospital is out of network, you may pay much more, and that spending may not count toward your out-of-pocket maximum. Some plan types offer little or no coverage outside the network except in emergencies. Always check whether your doctors and preferred hospitals are in the network before you decide, and remember that networks can change.

Second, prescriptions can change the math. Each plan has its own drug list, often called a formulary, with different tiers. The same drug may cost very different amounts depending on the plan and its tier. If you take a brand-name or specialty medicine, checking the drug list can make a big difference in your yearly cost.

Third, look at how specialists work on each plan. Some plans require a referral from your primary care doctor before you see a specialist. That is not a cost by itself, but it can affect how easily you get care and how much you might pay if a step is missed.

Finally, consider any financial help you may qualify for. Depending on your situation, savings on Marketplace plans may lower your premium or, in some cases, your out-of-pocket costs. Rules and amounts vary by income, household, state and year, so a licensed advisor can help you see what applies to you.

Putting It All Together

Once you have your list and a few plans to compare, here is a simple way to bring it all together.

  1. Write down each plan's monthly premium and multiply it by twelve to get your yearly premium.
  2. Estimate your care costs for an expected year using each plan's deductible, copays and coinsurance.
  3. Add the yearly premium and expected care costs to get a likely total for each plan.
  4. Add the yearly premium and the out-of-pocket maximum to see each plan's worst-case total.
  5. Check that your doctors, hospitals and prescriptions work with each plan you are considering.
  6. Choose the plan that fits both your budget and your comfort level with risk.

The best plan for you is not always the cheapest each month or the richest in benefits. It is the one that balances what you can afford to pay every month with what you could handle if the year does not go as planned.
