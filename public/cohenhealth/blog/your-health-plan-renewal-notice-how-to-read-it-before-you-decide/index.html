Your Health Plan Renewal Notice: How to Read It Before You Decide

6 min read · Updated October 16, 2026

Every fall, many people with individual or family health insurance get a letter or email from their plan. It might be called a renewal notice, a notice of change, or something similar. It is easy to glance at it, see that your coverage will continue, and set it aside.

That can be a costly habit. Plans can change from one year to the next, sometimes in small ways and sometimes in big ones. Your own life may have changed too. Taking some time with your renewal notice now can help you decide whether to keep your plan or look at other options before the next enrollment window closes.

This guide walks you through what to look for, step by step, in plain language.

Why Your Renewal Notice Matters

A renewal notice is your plan's way of telling you what next year will look like if you stay put. It usually covers your new premium, any changes to your benefits, and sometimes changes to the network of doctors and hospitals.

Here is the key point: if you do nothing, many plans renew automatically. Auto-renewal can be convenient, but it also means you accept whatever changes are coming, whether they work for you or not. In some cases, a plan may be discontinued entirely and you may be moved into a different plan that the insurer picks for you.

If you get help paying for a Marketplace plan, your savings may also be recalculated for the new year. Your income, household size, and the plans available in your area can all affect that amount. Exact rules and deadlines vary by state and year, so it is worth confirming your details with a licensed advisor.

The Five Things to Check First

You do not need to read every line of fine print to get a clear picture. Start with these five areas.

  1. Your monthly premium. Compare next year's amount with what you pay now. Even if the change looks modest, think about what it adds up to over a full year.
  2. Your deductible. This is how much you pay for many covered services before your plan starts sharing costs. A higher deductible can mean more out of your pocket early in the year.
  3. Your out-of-pocket maximum. This is the most you would pay for covered, in-network care in a plan year. It is your safety net if something big happens.
  4. Copays and coinsurance. Look at what you will pay for common visits, such as primary care, specialists, urgent care, and generic prescriptions.
  5. The provider network. Check whether your doctors, the hospitals you prefer, and your pharmacy are still listed as in-network for next year.

If any of these changed in a way that worries you, that is your signal to compare other plans rather than simply renewing.

Do Not Skip the Network and Drug List

Two of the most overlooked changes are also two of the most important: the provider network and the list of covered drugs, often called a formulary.

Networks can shrink or shift. A doctor you have seen for years might leave the network, or a nearby hospital might no longer be included. Your renewal notice may not spell this out clearly, so it helps to look up each provider you rely on in the plan's directory for the coming year. Calling the doctor's office to confirm is a smart extra step.

Drug lists can change too. A medicine you take regularly could move to a different cost tier, need prior approval, or be dropped. Make a simple list of every prescription you and your family take, then check each one against next year's formulary.

For example, imagine a parent whose child sees an allergist and takes a daily inhaler. Before renewing, that parent would want to confirm the allergist is still in-network and see how the inhaler is covered next year. A few minutes of checking can prevent an unpleasant surprise at the pharmacy counter in January.

Think About How Your Life Has Changed

Your renewal notice tells you how your plan changed. It does not tell you how your life changed. That part is up to you.

Ask yourself a few honest questions:

  • Did you get married, divorced, or have a baby this year?
  • Did your income go up or down, or did you start working for yourself?
  • Are you planning a surgery, a pregnancy, or ongoing treatment next year?
  • Did you move, or are you spending more time in another state?
  • Are you seeing new specialists or taking new medications?
  • Did you barely use your coverage this year, or did you use it a lot?

If you expect to need more care next year, a plan with a lower deductible and out-of-pocket maximum might be worth a closer look, even if the premium is higher. If you are generally healthy and rarely see a doctor, a different balance might fit better. There is no one right answer. The best plan is the one that matches how you actually use health care.

If your income has changed and you have a Marketplace plan, updating your information can matter. Reporting changes can affect the help you receive, so it is wise to review this with an advisor. Cohen Health advisors can explain how these pieces fit together, though for specific tax questions you should talk with a tax professional.

Renew, Switch, or Explore Something New

Once you have reviewed your notice and your own needs, you generally have three paths.

First, you can keep your current plan. If your doctors and drugs are still covered and the costs still make sense for your budget, renewing may be the simplest choice. Just make sure you understand the changes before you let it roll over.

Second, you can switch to a different plan of the same type. There may be options in your area that better fit your doctors, prescriptions, or budget for the coming year. New plans can also become available in some areas from year to year.

Third, you can look at a different kind of coverage altogether. Depending on your situation, that might include ACA Marketplace plans or private PPO plans. Each type works differently. Marketplace plans follow certain federal rules and may offer income-based help, while private PPO plans may offer broader networks for some people but can have their own eligibility requirements. Which one fits depends on your health needs, budget, location, and preferences.

A simple way to compare is to write down your must-haves before you start. That list might include specific doctors, a certain hospital, particular medications, or a maximum monthly budget. Then compare each option against that list instead of trying to weigh everything at once.

Timing matters here. Enrollment windows and deadlines vary by plan type, state, and year. If you are thinking about making a change, it is best to start early so you have time to ask questions and gather information without feeling rushed.

A Quick Renewal Checklist

Before you make your final decision, run through this short list:

  • I compared next year's premium, deductible, and out-of-pocket maximum with this year's.
  • I checked that my doctors, hospitals, and pharmacy are in-network for next year.
  • I looked up every prescription on next year's drug list.
  • I thought about any life changes and the care I expect to need.
  • I updated my income and household information if needed.
  • I know the enrollment deadline that applies to me.
  • I compared at least one or two other options before deciding.

If you can check every box, you are in a strong position to make a confident choice.
