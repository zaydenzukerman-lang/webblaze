Can Owner-Operators Deduct Health Insurance? What the IRS Actually Says

6 min read · Updated October 17, 2026

Generally, yes — if you're an owner-operator running your own business as a sole proprietor, you can deduct your health insurance premiums. The mechanics of how, and how much, are where most people get it wrong.

The deduction, in plain terms

The self-employed health insurance deduction (from IRC Section 162(l)) lets qualifying self-employed people deduct the premiums they paid for medical, dental, vision, and some long-term care insurance for themselves, their spouse, and their dependents. It's an adjustment to your income, not a line item on Schedule C itself.

One technical update worth knowing: this used to be calculated on a worksheet inside IRS Publication 535. That publication hasn't been revised since the 2022 tax year. The deduction now has its own form — Form 7206, Self-Employed Health Insurance Deduction — filed alongside Form 1040. If you're reading an older guide that cites Pub. 535, it's out of date.

Does this apply to owner-operators?

For most, yes. An owner-operator contracting independently and reporting business income on Schedule C as a sole proprietor is exactly who Form 7206 is built for. A W-2 company driver, by contrast, is a common-law employee with no self-employment net profit — not eligible for this deduction at all. It's the same 1099-vs-W2 line that affects the rest of your benefits picture.

The rule that trips people up

You can't count any month you were eligible to participate in an employer-subsidized health plan — yours, your spouse's, or even a dependent or child-under-27's employer plan — even if you didn't actually enroll in it. This one catches drivers whose spouse has a W-2 job with benefits available to the household, even if the family never used that plan.

How much can you actually deduct?

The deduction is capped at the net profit from the specific business that set up the plan, after subtracting the deductible half of your self-employment tax and any retirement-plan contributions tied to that business. It can't exceed that number, and it can't create or increase a business loss.

One detail that surprises people: this deduction lowers your income tax, but it does not reduce the net earnings figure used to calculate your self-employment tax. The IRS is explicit about that — don't expect it to shrink your Social Security and Medicare tax bill.

Where it actually goes on your return

It's an above-the-line adjustment to income, claimed on Schedule 1, Line 17 — not an itemized deduction, so you get it whether or not you itemize. You can't also use the same premiums for the Schedule A medical-expense deduction; no double-dipping between the two.

What it can cover

The deduction covers you, your spouse, your dependents, and a child under 27 at year-end even if that child isn't claimed as a dependent on your return. Qualified long-term care premiums count too, up to an age-based cap that the IRS adjusts every year — check the current Form 7206 instructions at IRS.gov for the exact figure before you file.

If you bought your plan through the Marketplace

If a premium tax credit was involved, the calculation gets meaningfully more complex — the IRS directs filers to Publication 974 for a required special computation. This is exactly the kind of situation worth handing to a tax professional rather than working out on your own.

Common questions

Does this replace talking to a tax preparer? No. This is general education, not personalized tax advice. Your specific business structure, other income sources, and any Marketplace subsidy can all change the actual math.

What if I run my business as an S-corp instead of a sole proprietorship? Different mechanics apply — the corporation has to pay or reimburse the premiums and run them through your W-2 wages for you to qualify. That's a materially different setup from a simple sole proprietorship, and worth a conversation with your accountant before you restructure anything.
