Family Coverage

Kid Away at College? Make Sure Your Family Plan Travels Too

6 min read · Updated October 22, 2026

Fall is a busy time for families with college students. Midterms are underway, holiday travel plans are taking shape, and open enrollment for next year's health coverage is right around the corner. If your son or daughter is living away from home, this is a great moment to ask a simple question: does our health plan actually work where my kid is living?

Many parents assume that if their child is on the family plan, they are covered anywhere. Sometimes that is true. Sometimes it is only partly true. The answer depends on how your plan's network is set up and where your student goes to school. Let's walk through what to check so you can make a smart choice for the coming year.

Why Distance Matters More Than You Might Think

Every health plan has a network. That is the group of doctors, hospitals, labs and pharmacies the plan works with. When you stay inside the network, you usually pay less. When you go outside it, you may pay more, or the plan may not pay at all except in an emergency.

Here is where college comes in. Some plans have networks that cover a wide area, even across many states. Others are built around one local region. If your family lives in one state and your student goes to school several hours away, or across the country, their nearby doctors and urgent care centers may not be in your plan's network.

A few common situations:

  • A plan with a local network may only cover emergencies when your student is out of the area. A sore throat, a sprained ankle or a follow-up visit might not be covered the way you expect.
  • A plan with a broader network may let your student see doctors near campus at in-network prices.
  • Some plans require a referral from a primary care doctor before seeing a specialist. That can be tricky if the primary care doctor is back home.

Emergencies are generally treated differently than routine care, but what counts as an emergency, and how follow-up care is handled afterward, can vary. It is worth reading the details rather than guessing.

Questions to Ask About Your Current Plan

Before open enrollment, take a few minutes to look at your current plan through your student's eyes. You can find much of this in your plan documents or by calling the plan's member services line. A licensed advisor can also help you sort it out.

  1. Is the network local, regional or national? Look for clinics, urgent care centers and a hospital near campus.
  2. Does the plan cover any non-emergency care outside the network area? If so, how much does your student pay?
  3. Are referrals required to see specialists? If yes, how would that work from a distance?
  4. Is there a pharmacy near campus that is in network? Can prescriptions be filled by mail?
  5. Does the plan include telehealth visits, and can your student use them from another state?
  6. If your student takes a regular medication or sees a therapist or specialist, can that care continue near school?

If the answers make you nervous, that does not mean you need to panic. It just means this year's open enrollment is a good time to compare options.

The Campus Health Center and School Health Plans

Most colleges have a student health center. These clinics can be very handy for everyday needs like colds, minor injuries and basic checkups. But a campus clinic is not the same as health insurance. It may not handle lab work, imaging, specialists or hospital care. If your student needs any of those, your health plan is what pays for them.

Many schools also offer a student health plan. Some schools even require students to either buy the school plan or prove they have other coverage, often through a waiver process. Rules, costs and benefits vary a lot from school to school and year to year.

A school plan might make sense when:

  • Your family plan has a local network that does not reach the campus area.
  • Your student will be at school most of the year, including summers.
  • The school plan's network includes the doctors and hospitals near campus.

Keeping your student on the family plan might make more sense when:

  • Your plan has a broad network that includes providers near campus.
  • Your student comes home often and sees family doctors during breaks.
  • Adding them to your plan works out better once you look at total costs, not just premiums.

There is no one right answer. The best choice depends on your plan, your student's school and your student's health needs. Before you decide, compare what each option covers, what it costs over a full year, and how easy it is to use from where your student actually lives.

How Open Enrollment Fits In

Open enrollment is the yearly window when you can change your health plan for the next year without needing a special reason. Dates vary depending on where you live and what kind of coverage you have, so check your state's timeline or ask an advisor.

If you buy your own coverage, whether a Marketplace plan or a private plan, this is your chance to pick a plan with a network that fits your whole family, including a student who lives elsewhere. Some families find a plan with a broader network works better. Others look at separate coverage for the student. A licensed advisor can show you what is available where you live and where your student goes to school.

If you get coverage through work, your employer's open enrollment is when you can switch plan options. Look at whether your employer offers more than one network type. One option might travel better than another.

Also keep in mind that most students can stay on a parent's plan until they turn 26, as long as the plan offers dependent coverage. If your student is getting close to that age, start planning now so there is no gap later.

Have the Coverage Talk Over Fall Break

Young adults are often handling health care on their own for the first time. A short conversation when they are home for a weekend or a holiday can save a lot of stress later. Here are a few things to go over together:

  • Make sure your student has a copy of their insurance card, either the physical card or a photo on their phone.
  • Show them how to find an in-network urgent care center and pharmacy near campus.
  • Explain the difference between urgent care and the emergency room, and when each makes sense.
  • Walk through what a copay, deductible and coinsurance are, in simple terms.
  • Save the plan's member services number and any telehealth app in their phone.
  • Talk about what to do if they get a bill they do not understand. The answer can be as simple as "send me a photo and we will figure it out together."

Your student may also want to sign a form that lets you talk with doctors or the insurance company on their behalf, since privacy rules can limit what parents can see once a child is an adult. Ask your student's school or a legal professional which forms make sense for your family.

A little preparation now means that if your student wakes up with a high fever at midnight, they will know where to go and what to bring.
