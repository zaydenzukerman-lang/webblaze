The DOT Physical Blood Pressure Rule — and What It Has to Do With Your Health Insurance

6 min read · Updated October 17, 2026

There's one point worth clearing up before anything else, because it costs drivers real money every year: a DOT physical certifies that you're medically fit to drive. It pays nothing if you get sick, get hurt, or need ongoing care. It is not health insurance, and it was never meant to be.

What a DOT physical actually is

A DOT physical is a federally mandated fitness-to-drive safety screening under 49 CFR Part 391. Passing it gets you a Medical Examiner's Certificate confirming you're qualified to operate a commercial motor vehicle — a licensing and compliance requirement, not a benefit you're purchasing. It's typically paid out of pocket by the driver or employer as a regulatory cost, not billed to health insurance, and it isn't a substitute for having any.

The blood pressure numbers examiners actually use

The underlying regulation itself is fairly general — it disqualifies anyone with “a current clinical diagnosis of high blood pressure likely to interfere with his/her ability to operate a commercial motor vehicle safely.” The specific numbers examiners use to make that call come from FMCSA's Medical Examiner's Handbook:

  • Normal / well-controlled blood pressure: certified for up to the standard maximum of 24 months.
  • Stage 1 (140–159 systolic and/or 90–99 diastolic): certified for 1 year, with annual rechecks.
  • Stage 2 (160–179 systolic and/or 100–109 diastolic): a one-time 3-month certification to start or adjust treatment and bring it down to 140/90 or less — then up to 1 year once it's controlled and the treatment is well tolerated.
  • Stage 3 (180 systolic and/or 110 diastolic, or higher): not qualified, even temporarily, until blood pressure is brought down to 140/90 or less with treatment that's well tolerated — only then can a 6-month certificate be issued.

Any single reading over 139/89 has to be confirmed with a second measurement later in the same exam before it's used to make a certification decision — so one high number taken under exam-room stress isn't automatically disqualifying.

A caveat worth knowing

FMCSA's thresholds are based on an older clinical standard from 1997, not the newer 2017 guidelines many doctors' offices use today (which label 130–139/80–89 as “stage 1 hypertension”). If your regular doctor mentions a different stage than your medical examiner does, that's why — for DOT certification purposes specifically, the numbers above are what actually apply.

It's not a permanent bar — it's “fix it, then get recertified”

Except at the highest stage, this is a graduated system built around bringing your numbers down, not a one-strike disqualification. Stage 3 is the one exception: there's no short-term grace certificate at that level — treatment has to actually work before you're certified again, even briefly.

Why this is actually a health insurance question

Managing blood pressure long-term — medication, follow-up visits, adjusting treatment that isn't working — is exactly the kind of ongoing care an actual health plan covers, not a once-a-year (or once-every-two-years) exam. FMCSA's own guidance even notes that examiners have to weigh whether a driver's blood pressure medication itself causes drowsiness that could affect safe driving. Staying certified isn't just about the exam day; it's about having real coverage for the care that keeps your numbers where they need to be between exams.

Common questions

Does my health plan cover the cost of the DOT physical itself? Generally no — it's typically paid directly by the driver or employer as a compliance cost, separate from health insurance.

I got a 3-month certification because of my blood pressure — what now? Get a real treatment plan in place before that window closes. This is exactly where having actual health coverage, not just the DOT certificate, matters.

Can high blood pressure permanently disqualify me from driving? Only at the highest stage, and even then only until it's brought under control with treatment you can tolerate — there's no indefinite bar once that happens.
