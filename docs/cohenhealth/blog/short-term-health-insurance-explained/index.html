Short-Term Health Insurance: What It Actually Covers (and Doesn’t)

8 min read · Updated September 15, 2026

Short-term health insurance gets pitched as a quick, cheap fix for a coverage gap — and it can be exactly that. It can also be a real problem if you buy it thinking it's a substitute for full major medical coverage, because it isn't, by design. Here's what it actually is, what it typically covers and excludes, how the application process actually works, and the specific situations where it's genuinely the right tool.

What Short-Term Insurance Actually Is

Short-term (technically "short-term limited-duration insurance," or STLDI) plans are medically underwritten, meaning the carrier asks health questions and can decline you or exclude a condition, similar to the underwriting process for private PPO plans described elsewhere on this site. Where they differ sharply is duration and benefit design: short-term plans are built to run for a matter of months, not indefinitely, and federal rules cap and structure how long they can run and renew, though the specifics vary by state.

Because they're not required to meet the ACA's essential-health-benefits standard, short-term plans routinely exclude or limit categories of care that major medical plans are required to cover — this is the single most important thing to understand before buying one.

How the Application Actually Works, Step by Step

  1. Pick a coverage start and end date based on your actual gap — most people underestimate how long they'll need it, so add a buffer if your timeline is uncertain.
  2. Answer the health questionnaire completely and honestly — the same underwriting accuracy rules apply here as with any medically underwritten plan.
  3. Get your decision, which is often fast — many short-term applications are decided within a day or two given the simpler, leaner nature of the product.
  4. Read the actual exclusions list before you pay the first premium, not after you need to use the plan — this is the step people skip most often.
  5. Calendar the plan's actual end date and start your next-step application (Marketplace, employer, or another bridge) well before it, since a short-term plan won't auto-convert into something else when it expires.

What's Typically Covered

Short-term plans generally do cover the core things people picture when they think "health insurance": doctor visits, urgent care, emergency room visits, and hospitalization for a new injury or illness that develops after the policy starts. For a healthy person who just needs a bridge and wants protection against a worst-case accident or sudden illness, this can be genuinely adequate coverage for the gap it's meant to fill.

What's Typically Excluded or Limited

This is the part that catches people off guard, and it's worth reading closely before you buy:

  • Pre-existing conditions are typically excluded entirely, not just rated up — if you have a condition already, a short-term plan may not cover care related to it at all, for the life of the policy.
  • Prescription drug coverage is often limited or absent, especially for maintenance medications for ongoing conditions.
  • Maternity care is routinely excluded outright — this is one of the most common surprises for people who assumed some level of coverage existed.
  • Mental health and substance use treatment coverage is frequently limited compared to what an ACA-compliant plan is required to provide.
  • Annual and lifetime maximums may apply, meaning the plan stops paying after a certain dollar amount — something ACA-compliant major medical plans are barred from doing.

When Short-Term Actually Makes Sense

Short-term coverage tends to be the right tool for a genuinely short, well-defined gap: between jobs for a month or two while waiting for new employer coverage to start, right after aging off a parent's plan while a Marketplace application processes, or during a brief window between other coverage. It's a purpose-built bridge, and used as one, it does its job.

When It's the Wrong Tool

It stops being the right tool the moment it's used as a long-term substitute for real coverage, particularly for anyone with an ongoing health condition, anyone who might need maternity care during the policy period, or anyone using it repeatedly back-to-back to avoid a "real" plan's underwriting or cost. If you find yourself renewing a short-term plan again and again, that's usually a sign it's time to price out a private PPO plan or a Marketplace plan properly instead.

Short-Term vs. Private PPO: The Difference That Actually Matters

These two get confused constantly because both are medically underwritten and both can start quickly. The real difference is what happens after approval. A private PPO plan, once you're approved, functions as ordinary major medical coverage — no expiration date built in, no reduced-benefit design, ACA-style protections don't apply the same way but the plan itself is a standard PPO. A short-term plan is designed to expire and is built with a leaner benefit set from day one. If someone offers you a short-term plan and calls it equivalent to a private PPO plan, that's worth double-checking directly, because the two are not interchangeable.

Common Myths About Short-Term Plans

  • Myth: short-term plans are just cheaper versions of regular insurance. Fact: they're a different product entirely, with different benefit rules and a built-in expiration — not simply a discounted version of major medical coverage.
  • Myth: short-term plans have to cover the same essential benefits as ACA plans. Fact: they're specifically exempt from that requirement, which is exactly why premiums are lower and exclusions are more common.
  • Myth: I can just keep renewing a short-term plan indefinitely as my regular coverage. Fact: renewal limits vary by state, and even where renewal is allowed, using it as permanent coverage usually means going without protections a major medical plan would provide.
  • Myth: short-term plans don't do medical underwriting. Fact: they do — health questions, possible declines, and possible exclusions all apply, the same underwriting logic used across medically underwritten products.

Real-World Scenarios

Someone starting a new job in six weeks, healthy, with no ongoing conditions and no plans for a pregnancy in that window, is a reasonable candidate for a short-term plan to bridge exactly that gap.

Someone with a chronic condition requiring regular prescriptions should be cautious about a short-term plan covering that condition at all — a Marketplace plan's guaranteed issue and essential-health-benefits requirements are usually the safer bridge.

A couple actively trying to conceive should assume a short-term plan won't cover maternity care and plan Marketplace or employer coverage accordingly before that becomes relevant.

Someone who has already renewed a short-term plan twice, back to back, is a good candidate to stop and properly compare Marketplace and private PPO options instead of renewing a third time.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Can a short-term plan be renewed indefinitely? Rules vary significantly by state — some cap total duration including renewals, others restrict it further. This is genuinely one of the more state-specific areas of insurance, worth confirming directly for where you live.
  • Does short-term coverage count as "creditable coverage" for tax or other purposes? Generally not in the same way ACA-compliant coverage does — this is a real difference from major medical plans and worth asking about directly if it matters for your situation.
  • Is short-term insurance cheaper than a Marketplace plan? Usually, yes, on premium — but that's largely because the benefit design is leaner and underwriting screens out or excludes higher-risk applicants and conditions. Cheaper premium and better value aren't automatically the same thing.
  • Can I be declined for a short-term plan? Yes — like any underwritten plan, a carrier can decline an application, exclude specific conditions, or approve with a modified rate.
  • What happens if I get seriously ill while on a short-term plan? Coverage applies to new conditions that arise during the policy period, subject to the plan's specific terms and any applicable maximums — read the actual policy for how a serious new diagnosis would be handled, since this varies by carrier.

Why the Rules Vary So Much by State

Short-term insurance is one of the more state-specific corners of health coverage, because states retain real authority over how long a short-term policy can run, whether and how many times it can be renewed, and in some cases whether short-term plans can be sold in that state at all. A plan design that's routine in one state may be capped much shorter, or unavailable entirely, in another. This isn't a minor footnote — it's the reason two people in different states asking "how long can I keep a short-term plan" can get genuinely different correct answers, and it's exactly why confirming your own state's specific rules before buying is worth the extra step rather than assuming a national standard applies.

This state-level variation is also why a short-term plan bought while living in one state doesn't necessarily behave the same way if you move mid-policy — a cross-state move during a short-term policy period is worth a direct call to the carrier, not an assumption that coverage simply continues unchanged.

The Short Version

  • Short-term plans are medically underwritten and built to run for a limited period, not indefinitely — that expiration is the core design feature, not an accident.
  • They typically cover new injuries and illnesses well but routinely exclude pre-existing conditions, maternity care, and often limit prescription and mental health coverage.
  • They're not required to meet ACA essential-health-benefit standards, which is exactly why premiums are lower and exclusions are more common.
  • They make the most sense for a short, well-defined gap — not as a repeated substitute for major medical coverage.
  • Rules on duration and renewal vary meaningfully by state — confirm your own state's specifics before assuming a plan works the way a friend's did elsewhere.

A Short Glossary

  • Short-term limited-duration insurance (STLDI): the formal name for a short-term health plan, medically underwritten and built to run for a limited period rather than indefinitely.
  • Essential health benefits: a set of coverage categories ACA-compliant plans are required to include — short-term plans are specifically exempt from this requirement.
  • Pre-existing condition exclusion: a policy provision that excludes coverage for a condition you had before the policy started, common on short-term plans and rare on ACA-compliant ones.
  • Lifetime maximum: a total dollar cap on what a plan will ever pay out — barred on ACA-compliant major medical plans, but sometimes present on short-term plans.

The Actual Next Step

Treat a short-term plan as a bridge with a specific end date in mind, not an open-ended answer. A licensed advisor can tell you plainly whether your specific gap is genuinely a short-term situation, or whether a private PPO or Marketplace plan is the better fit once your health, timeline, and budget are actually laid out.
